What the three numbers mean

HARD — scratch hardness, the Mohs idea. It decides who marks whom: only a harder thing scratches a softer one, and the margin decides how much comes off. Two things of equal hardness barely mark each other however hard you swing.
BIND — the work it takes to free one cubic metre of the stuff. This is toughness, and it is not hardness. Flesh is soft (hardness 0.5) and binds hard: you cannot crumble it. Glass is hard (5.5) and binds weakly: it shatters. Bind is why a diamond can cut a rope badly and a blunt rock can break a window.
MOLECULE — the smallest piece that can come off, in millimetres. It is dust: iron at half a millimetre, rock sand at two. The threshold to free one molecule is bind × its volume, which is what lets one table span a scratched blade and a shot star.

The same material breaks at different sizes

Wood is carbon, hydrogen and oxygen with a little nitrogen — but you almost never meet wood as its molecule. Saw it and you get dust. Chop it and you get splinters and sticks. Throw it against a wall and you get logs. Same material, same table; the interaction decides which size the pieces come off in. That is the breaks into column, and the readout under the interaction tells you what you would actually be holding.

Does everything really collide as grains?

No — and that is a large saving. Three tiers, and only the middle needs grain-by-grain contact:

WHOLE — rock, metal, wood, ice, bone, a star. Never grains because they are never grains: one record with a wear counter, and grains exist only once liberated. Most of the world by volume.
GRANULAR — sand, gravel, ash, soot, powder, snow, salt, gunpowder. The only tier that needs real grain collision: piling, holding an angle of repose, pouring through a gap.
FLUID & GAS — water, oil, lava, acid, steam, smoke, plasma. No contacts at all; they move by density, which is one comparison and cheaper than contact.
